# Space Combat Simulation — Decision Log

Why things are the way they are. Add an entry whenever a decision changes or a design question is settled,
**newest at the top of the table**. Entries are never edited away: a superseded decision and the reason it
was superseded are both worth reading.

The rows below the most recent few are not strictly in date order — the log was appended to before that
convention existed. Left as it is rather than re-sorted, since the dates are on every row and shuffling
history to look tidier is its own small dishonesty.

The design itself is in [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md), and what is still open is in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md).

**Three documents, split by why you would read them.** Section numbers are global and stable across all
three, so a reference such as "§12" means the same section wherever it is written — which is why the numbering
inside any one file is not contiguous.

| 2026-09-09 | A thruster must have structure against the face opposite its nozzle, or `blueprintProblem` rejects the layout. An engine is bolted to the ship at the end it pushes from and exhausts out of the other; turn one round and it is held on by its nozzle, with the mounting in the exhaust and the thrust delivered to nothing. This looks like it contradicts §12, which says a thruster firing into its own hull should be penalised continuously and explicitly *not* rejected, because a hard edge is one a mutation cannot cross and the search wants a gradient. It does not, and the distinction is worth keeping: §12 is about **obstruction**, which is continuous — how much of a plume is blocked — while this is about **attachment**, which is discrete however it is modelled, since a mount is either on the hull or it is not. §12 was narrowed rather than overruled, and the continuous case it was really about is still open. Implemented by nudging the module forward along its facing by the attachment tolerance and asking whether it now overlaps, which reuses the separating-axis test and so is correct at any mounting angle without a face-contact test of its own; a neighbour merely alongside is unmoved by a forward nudge and correctly does not count, and nor does one touching at a corner. Only `structure` counts as something to push against — engines and guns are not load paths. This also brings §12's abutment tolerance into existence early, as `ATTACHMENT_TOLERANCE`, a centimetre: exact abutment is a knife edge that only a layout drawn on round numbers lands on, and a hand-edited file will not. Connectivity should share that constant rather than introduce a second. The existing ray-cast test survives and is not redundant — the new rule catches a mount turned round and says nothing about a correctly mounted engine whose plume hits something further aft. |
| 2026-09-09 | Blueprints are JSON files, parsed by `sim/blueprintFile.ts`, and the three authored ships are stored in the format the editor will read and write — settling the question §12 had been holding until the editor arrived. The conversion had one hard requirement and met it: **the golden checksums did not move**, which is the only evidence worth having that a rewrite of how ships are described changed nothing about what they are. Angles are degrees in the file and radians in `sim/`, converted as `(d / 180) * PI` — deliberately that ordering, since it is the one that round-trips exactly most often, and 90° must produce the same bits as `HALF_PI` or every derived figure shifts in the last place for no visible reason. Nothing derived is stored, so a corrected scaling law reaches old files instead of being frozen into them. Three things fell out. The parser **rejects unknown keys** rather than ignoring them, because `"barrel": 8` parses cleanly into a single-barrelled turret and nothing downstream can tell the author asked for eight — a silently different ship being worse than a refusal. It **delegates geometry to `blueprintProblem`** so there is one definition of a valid ship rather than two that drift, and the shipped ships go through the same parser a stranger's file does, so that path cannot rot unnoticed. And the files are **imported rather than fetched**, which keeps Node and the bundle on one route with no asynchrony; that needed `resolveJsonModule`, and it tightened the architecture test rather than loosening it — a relative import may now end `.json` as well as `.js`, but a `.json` one must carry `with { type: 'json' }`, which Node requires and whose absence type-checks and bundles perfectly happily before throwing at run time. The prose that would have been lost is now `notes` on the blueprint and on individual modules, carried through the format: the corvette's wings still explain that they hold the manoeuvring thrusters out where the moment arm is worth having and foul the bow gun doing it. |
| 2026-09-09 | **No abstract cost value, anywhere** (§2). A ship costs three real things: the materials making up its dry mass, the time to build it — from a module-complexity metric deliberately left unpinned — and the propellant and raw materials it burns running. Everything that wanted a points value is denominated in those instead. Three consequences propagated. §8 step 5's "fixed fleet budget" becomes a budget of *materials*, which the multiplayer notes had already reached independently: they prefer "starting resources on a mothership with build priorities" over a pre-built fleet, and that preference is the same idea. §12's requirement that materials be "priced, not merely better" keeps its force but changes meaning — a better material is scarcer, slower to work, or heavier, rather than carrying a number. And the blueprint editor gets no cost line: dry mass is not a placeholder standing in until a cost model arrives, it *is* the materials currency, permanently, which is a better thing for the readout to say. Worth noting what is not yet live: build time only bites once ships are built during a battle (§8 step 6), and running costs need a fuel model, so for everything up to and including v1 skirmish the whole of cost really is mass. The three-currency model is the destination, not the current state. |
| 2026-09-09 | A turret's barrels spread evenly right across the mount face, `n` of them making `n + 1` equal gaps, so there is one whole gap outboard of each end barrel. This replaces a spacing of five calibres, which was chosen for looks and had two problems: nothing in the formula stopped a larger value overhanging the mount, and at 18% of the face the row was too narrow to read as separate firing positions at all. The new rule takes no constant — the face and the barrel count fix the spacing between them — and cannot overhang, since the row spans `(n-1)/(n+1)` of the face, under one for every `n`. Worth being clear that this pins a constant rather than abolishing one: the spacing it gives, in calibres, is `25n/(n+1)`, so it is very nearly the old rule with 25 in place of 5, and the merit is that 25 is derived rather than defended. The face is the *smaller* of the two dimensions, because a turret traverses: at rest the row lies across the width, ninety degrees round it lies along the length, and a mount wider than it is long would otherwise sweep its barrels through whatever is beside it — taking the lesser makes the footprint the inscribed circle, which is what a barbette is. It also settles a confusion in the prose rather than in the numbers. The calibre rule and the spacing rule look related and are not: barrels occupy about 4% of their own pitch, so `calibre ∝ 1/n` was never packing, and it is now documented as what it is — a *budget*, saying a mount of a given size is worth the same weight of metal downrange however it is arranged. Two things were checked rather than assumed before widening the row. Accuracy: the barrels fire parallel and never converge, so the offset is a permanent miss distance, but the duel and swarm hit counts are unchanged, targets being 16–17 m against a 4.7 m row — §12 records harmonisation as the answer if that stops being true. Recoil: it is applied as a pure linear impulse at the centre of mass with no torque term, so an offset barrel costs nothing, which is an existing simplification independent of this choice. |
| 2026-09-05 | The Pages deploy §9 had been holding back is live, its trigger — a viewer worth showing — having fired. It is a job in `ci.yml` gated on the typecheck, test and browser jobs, because the published page is the one people are handed and must never be a build that failed its own tests. The alternative considered was committing `dist/index.html`, which does not actually work: GitHub serves raw files as `text/plain` with `nosniff`, so a raw link shows minified source rather than a running game — all of the cost of a build artefact in the tree and none of the benefit. Building in the job means the link cannot go stale. One trap worth recording: naming any scope in a job's `permissions` sets every unnamed scope to `none`, so `contents: read` has to be listed explicitly or the checkout fails. |
| 2026-09-05 | `npm run dev` rebuilds the viewer on save, and a README says how to run the thing. The build script previously declared that it had no watch mode and should not grow one, citing §9's anti-recommendation; that was drawn in the wrong place. | 2026-09-05 | Turrets draw as three layers — the module box, the sector the barrel can sweep, then the barrel — and what makes all three legible is the ordering of *values*, not of hues. The sweep is a pale wash so it shows over the dark field as much as over the hull, and the barrel is dark so it shows against that wash. Drawing the sweep as a dark tint was tried first and was the obvious-looking mistake: it only existed where it had something light to darken, so a sector reaching out past the hull — which is most of it — was invisible against the background. A barrel never leaves its own sector, since the two share a radius, so it only has to contrast with the sweep, which is what lets it be the darkest thing on the ship. The sweep is deliberately not team-tinted: it marks where a gun *could* point rather than anything belonging to the ship, and colouring it by team made it read as hull. Drawn at three times the barrel's length rather than at the barrel's own reach — the quantity being shown is an *angle*, and at the barrel's length the wedge is too small to judge and reads as the volume the barrel occupies rather than the sky it covers — it turns §3's derived firing arcs into something visible per mount — the corvette's bow gun shows a narrow wedge because its own wings foul it, and the gunship's beam mounts show full discs because they are held clear of the spine, which are exactly the trades those layouts were authored to make and were previously only claimed in a comment. Exhaust moved to a pass of its own after the turrets, because sharing the module pass put a burning engine underneath a nearby mount's sector. |
| 2026-09-05 | The camera moves *with* what it is following. Easing toward where the ships are means always chasing them, and the faster they travel the further behind it sits — at speed the lag was most of a screen. Their mean velocity is now applied to the camera first, so their motion cancels exactly and the easing has only the residual to close: the formation spreading, or its centre drifting. It is the turrets' velocity feed-forward (§4) arriving a second time in a different place, and the same insight — correct for the motion you can predict, and keep the feedback loop for the part you cannot. The `dt` it takes is *simulated* seconds, not wall seconds, since at eight times speed the feed-forward has eight times as far to carry. The camera moved to `render/camera.ts` to get this under test: it is arithmetic over a snapshot and touches no canvas, so it runs in Node, and one test measures the lag with the feed-forward against the lag without rather than asserting a magic distance. |
| 2026-09-05 | Both authored ships had every one of their eight lateral thrusters mounted backwards, firing into the wing they were bolted to from 0.1 m away. It flew perfectly well: a thruster produces thrust whatever its nozzle is buried in, so the error had no effect the simulation could notice, and it survived from the day the layouts were written until the viewer started drawing exhaust plumes. Fixed by pushing the wing mounts inboard, which is what puts their exhaust in clear air; the layouts' capability is unchanged — identical accelerations and torques in both senses — so only the specific allocation moved, and with it the duel checksum. The authored blueprints now have a test that casts each exhaust ray and fails if it crosses another module. The general question of what a buried nozzle *should* cost is recorded in §12, and it is not cosmetic: free thrust with no penalty is exactly the exploit shape the scaling laws warn about, and evolution would find it. |
| 2026-09-05 | Engines show what they are doing. A thruster now draws a plume as wide as its exit, stretching with throttle, so the *area* is proportional to the force being produced — the quantity worth reading off a picture. A pleasing consequence falls out of the scaling laws rather than being arranged: thrust scales with exit area, so thrust per unit width is identical for every engine, and every engine therefore reaches the same plume length at full throttle. A bigger engine is a wider flame, not a longer one, which is what a shared exhaust velocity should look like. The snapshot carries throttles to make it possible, and the ordering the renderer relies on — that a design lists its thrusters and turrets in the order their modules appear — is now pinned by a test, since if it ever diverged a ship would show one engine's flame on another's mount and steer with the wrong thruster. |
| 2026-09-05 | The camera frames the ships alone. Wells are drawn but no longer framed: one matters to the eye while the ships are on opposite sides of it, and once they are past it, holding it in shot only pushes the fight away — so the ships are the subject and the well is scenery. Fitting tightly then exposed that easing alone loses the race whenever the scene spreads faster than the camera follows, which at eight times speed it does, and a ship ends up cut off at the edge. The two directions are therefore not symmetric: **widen at once, close in gently**, and afterwards pull the centre back until the bounds are inside the view. A frame that has already cropped is worse than one that moved abruptly. |
| 2026-09-05 | The duel stopped being a tidy head-on engagement in empty space, because a scenario that exercises nothing demonstrates nothing. Both ships now start facing across the engagement and carrying mostly-crossing velocity, and a gravity well sits off to one side. The hit rate fell from 22 of 22 to about 7 in 10, which is the honest number: **100% was an artefact of the geometry**, since nothing was crossing and the lead solution had nothing to solve. Peak turn rate more than doubled, so the braking law is visible. The well is placed beside the engagement rather than between the ships, so nothing passes near enough for the softening to matter, and sized at about 1.1 m/s² — half the gunship's own acceleration, enough to bend every trajectory and never enough to leave a ship unable to resist. Two things followed from watching it rather than from reasoning. The camera framed *projectiles*, so a single missed round flying on for 30 s dragged the view out to 5 km and shrank the battle to nothing; bounds are now ships and wells, never rounds. And a well drawn nowhere is a physics bug to the eye — trajectories curving for no visible reason — so the renderer draws the radius at which each well pulls at 1 m/s², and the camera keeps it in shot. Zoom and pan were added with it, since auto-framing an arena that now includes a well leaves the ships small. |

| 2026-09-05 | Slice 0 finished with a Canvas2D viewer, and the duel moved out of the test fixtures to `scenarios/duel.ts` so the viewer and the golden checksum run *the same code* rather than two copies of a load-bearing step order — a viewer with its own copy would drift from what is pinned without anything failing, leaving what is on screen outside the checksum's cover. | 2026-09-05 | Rounds inherit the motion of the mount they left. `fireFrom` gave a round the hull's linear velocity only, so a gun 35 m off the centre of mass fired as though its muzzle were sitting still whenever the ship was turning — up to 4.4 m/s of tangential velocity dropped, at hull rates the pilot actually reaches. `FiringSolution` now carries `ω × r` at the muzzle and the round leaves with it. The gunnery solution had the *same* omission, which is why it was invisible: aim and physics agreed with each other while both ignored the mount's motion, so the error was a wrong model rather than a bias. Correcting only one would have introduced the bias, so `aimAt` now solves the lead from the muzzle's velocity too — placing the muzzle from the bearing the barrel holds now, since the bearing being solved for would be circular, which keeps the barrel's length in the answer. Recoil is unchanged and deliberately so: the round leaves with tangential velocity it already had while attached, so the charge does not push back for it. |
| 2026-09-05 | Ships built and fighting, which closes Slice 0's simulation half. Four things worth keeping. A ship's wrench is decided before the world advances but **applied from a force provider**, because `World` clears forces at every evaluation and evaluates twice on a primed step — so the provider replays a stored value and is idempotent by construction, and `Turrets.step` grew an optional buffer to collect its hull reaction the same way rather than writing into forces about to be cleared. **A salvo recoils once, not once per gun**: `fireFrom` gives a round the hull's velocity, so applying each gun's recoil as it fires lets the later rounds inherit a hull the earlier ones already pushed, and the broadside gains momentum invented by the firing order — caught by a test, which also pinned what *is* exact, since a round's mass comes from a magazine outside the mass budget (§12 ammunition). The pilot's range-band law had to **taper its closing speed** rather than run at full approach speed to the edge of the band, which produced a limit cycle across it instead of a settled station. And the turret braking law moved to `math.brakingRate`, since a pilot holding a heading is the same problem at hull scale; the turret suite passing unchanged is what says the extraction was behaviour-neutral. |
| 2026-09-05 | Four things recorded in §12 rather than built. **Turret arcs**: traverse limit and firing permission are different quantities — a gun can sweep past superstructure it must not shoot through — so firing permission is a *set* of bearing intervals, not a half-width, and `firingArc`'s `min` over obstructions is pessimistic twice over. **Authored data**: blueprints and fixtures move to files with the blueprint editor, because the editor's save format is the file format; the move needs a validator and a loader outside `sim/`, and costs the prose that explains each layout's trade. **Materials**: the scaling laws' *form* is a law but their *coefficients* are steel and a chemical propellant, so coefficients belong to a per-module material, while a genuinely different technology (a railgun) is a new archetype rather than a new material — with the caveat that materials must be priced or evolution simply takes the best one. **Connectivity**: §4 requires a graph per hull but a blueprint expresses none, so it should be *derived* from which module boundaries touch — as a graph rather than a tree, with per-edge strength, recomputed only on a sever — and the abutment tolerance is a game parameter rather than an implementation detail. That one also exposed that `blueprintProblem` never requires a layout to be connected at all. |

| 2026-09-04 | Kinematic turrets built, replacing the archive's worst mechanism (GA-searched PD gains driving hinge motors, §10). Braking-limited slew with velocity feed-forward, no gains. Three findings recorded in §4, each of which cost an attempt: the braking rate must be the *discrete* safe rate, since the continuous one overshoots, clamping the rate to stop that breaks the acceleration limit, and subtracting half a step leaves a dead band that a brisk mount parks inside; tracking needs feed-forward, without which a turret trails a moving target by a step of its angular motion and lags a rotating hull entirely; and turrets must be commanded *before* the world advances, or the slew and the hull's rotation cover different intervals. |
| 2026-09-04 | Recorded in §12 how thruster allocation extends, so it need not be re-derived: **gimbals** work by solving for the thrust vector rather than a scalar throttle, which keeps the wrench linear and turns the bound into a sector projection; **throttle rate limits** belong inside the solve as per-thruster bounds, never as post-processing, which would break the wrench; and **binary thrusters** belong after it as a duty cycle with delta-sigma modulation, since as a constraint they would make the problem mixed-integer and far harder rather than simpler. Also noted the one thing this constrains today: the throttles array is per ship and must persist between steps, so it cannot become shared scratch. |
| 2026-09-04 | Thruster allocation built. Three findings worth keeping: the torque row must be **preconditioned** (mount arms are metres, so torque outweighs force by ~10⁴ in the normal equations, and once thrusters pin the solve quietly fits torque and ignores force — 75% shortfall on achievable demands); the normal equations must **switch form** with the number of free thrusters, `A Aᵀ` when underdetermined and `Aᵀ A` when fewer than three remain, since the 3×3 form is singular there; and only the **worst violator** may be pinned per pass, because pinning all of them collapses a nine-thruster layout to two and never reconsiders. Together these took the worst-case shortfall from >100% to 5.4%, mean 0.016%. |
| 2026-09-03 | Tightened the commit model (§3), which had two holes. A craft could commit while already over a capital's interior and strike the citadel without meeting the armoured edge; and a craft that *moved* to the hull layer would stop being hittable by CIWS and lasers exactly when it should be most exposed. Fixed by making occupancy *added, never swapped* — a committed craft is in both layers — and by allowing occupancy to change only while clear of every hull, in either direction, which also governs waving off and gives "commit is arming" for free. Corrected the earlier claim that the transition is an event rather than a state: true of projectiles, false once commit exists, which is one bit per craft. Also corrected the unearned claim that committing draws more fire — every weapon is weapons-layer, so the real costs are a predictable terminal course and being collidable with turrets. |
| 2026-09-03 | Resolved how a torpedo reaches a hull (§3). A collision with a hull is a hull-layer event, projectile fire is a weapons-layer event, and nothing migrates between layers. A strafing run skims the deck; a terminal dive strikes it. So a kinetic-kill vehicle needs no warhead, "a torpedo is a fighter that crashes into things" becomes literal, and overfly-versus-commit becomes a doctrine choice priced in evasion. Ram versus dock needed no new mechanism — the §4 weld threshold already decides it. Capital hull-layer edge guns were kept as a separate open question, since torpedoes do not need them. |
