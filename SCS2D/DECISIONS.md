# Space Combat Simulation — Decision Log

Why things are the way they are. Add an entry whenever a decision changes or a design question is settled,
**newest at the top of the table**. Entries are never edited away: a superseded decision and the reason it
was superseded are both worth reading.

The rows below the most recent few are not strictly in date order — the log was appended to before that
convention existed. Left as it is rather than re-sorted, since the dates are on every row and shuffling
history to look tidier is its own small dishonesty.

The design itself is in [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md), and what is still open is in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md).

**Three documents, split by why you would read them.** Section numbers are global and stable across all
three, so a reference such as "§12" means the same section wherever it is written — which is why the numbering
inside any one file is not contiguous.

| 2026-09-24 | **A match plays on after the last kill while the goal counts, takes one ship, and can have a ghost for a goal.** A match ended the moment one entrant was left, and what was left of it was credited as though the survivor held its last state — which, for the goal, paid it for wherever the last kill happened to leave it: passing the goal at that instant was worth as much as reaching it, and being on the far side of a turn was worth as little as never coming back. It now ends early only when nothing more can be scored — nobody left, or one left with the race weighted at zero — and a match nobody is left in ends however many started it, so a lone ship shot out of the sky, or rammed into debris, stops. The price is time: on a small sample run eleven of twelve matches were decided and simulated time nearly doubled (760 s → 1440 s, 3.1 s → 4.8 s of wall time). **One ship to a match is allowed**, as a test of piloting alone: `Run` had refused any match of fewer than two and closed the generation unscored, and the page clamped the field to two. **The goal can be a ghost** (`goal.solid: false`): still a ship, since escorting finds its charge among ships, but its body is `ghost` — never indexed in the spatial grid, so no round, beam or plume finds it; skipped by collisions; not steered round by the ship keeping clear; and massless, so nothing moves it. Measured with the default 12 m marker it matters less than expected — across twelve matches two corvettes did the same damage either way and two gunships 16% more with a ghost — because a marker that size on a 500 m ring is seldom in the line of fire; it will count for more with a bigger marker or more ships to a match. No golden checksum moved, the flag being set by nothing but the match. |
| 2026-09-24 | **Breeding can move the face two modules share.** The seam drag's geometry moved from the editor to `sim/push.ts` (`sharedFace`, `shiftSeam`) so a mutation can use it: `moveSeam` picks a neighbour in the same list that is square to the module and joined along a face, and moves that face a grid step, one module growing as the other shrinks. It trades space — hull for engine — without the ship getting bigger, which otherwise takes a shrink and a grow that can each be refused. **The module that grows must have its whole face against the other**, so it only moves into space the other gives up. Without that rule the seam was refused 23–28% of the time, almost all of it the wider module growing past the edge of the narrower one into whatever sat beside it; with it, 5% on the shipped fleet and 3–4% on two-hundred-generation lineages, the remainder being modules welded only to the stretch of face the shrinking module gives up. | 2026-09-24 | **A moving face carries its neighbours, in the editor and in breeding alike, and two touching modules can trade a face.** Whatever sits against a face that moves — or is in the way of where it grows to — moves with it: pushed when the module grows, pulled when it shrinks. One rule, `pushNeighbours` in `sim/`, serves both callers, because the editor and evolution may not import each other and two rules would drift. **It spreads.** Whatever a moving placement would run into moves too, tested against the region it sweeps rather than its box so a module turned 15° is not missed; and so does whatever **hangs off** a moving placement — anything that reaches the resized module through welds only by way of something that is moving — so a spar pushed aside takes the engine on its end with it, where a spar alone would have slid along under the engine. Something with its own way back to the resized module stays put. It reaches only placements written in the same list — the layout, or one assembly's definition — since only those share a frame, with an instance moving as a whole. A copy of a shared part pushes among the copy's own neighbours, and one of a repeated row pushes nothing, its neighbours differing from copy to copy. In the editor it is the default and Ctrl (⌘) resizes the module alone. **Measured** by moving each face of every module one grid step, refusals for growth / shrinking: shipped fleet 59% / 34% without it, 43% / 35% pushing only the face's own neighbours, **22% / 12%** with the spread; two-hundred-generation lineages from the Dinky 58% / 51% → 30% / 34%, and from a bare core 49% / 42% → 16% / 20%. Most of what is left is the assembly boundary — a module inside a group growing into the hull outside it, 131 of the fleet's 167 growth refusals — and after that welds side by side sliding apart, since a pushed module moves along whatever it was welded to beside it. The rare overlap left at the top level is a loop: one instance both against the growing face and welded to what is behind the module, so pushing it drags the rest into the module being grown, which no push can resolve. **A push closes a gap before it crosses it**: what is in the way goes only as far as is left once the gap to it has closed, so it ends up touching whatever pushed it. The first cut moved it the whole distance, which keeps the gap — and since a drag re-applies the whole resize to the layout it started from at every step, the gap closed on one step and opened again on the next; on the corvette, pulling the bow in between its retro engines had the turret leap away from the engine pushing it and on into the other one. Measured the same way after that fix: growth / shrinking refused 18% / 11% on the shipped fleet, 25% / 33% from the Dinky and 8% / 13% from a bare core. **The seam**: with exactly two modules selected that are square to each other and joined along a face, a bar on that face moves it, one module growing as the other shrinks with both outer faces still and neither below the minimum size. It is offered only for two single modules and never for two copies of one shared part, where sizing one sizes the other. No golden checksum moved; evolution's seeded tests did not either. |
| 2026-09-24 | **A resize holds the opposite corner or edge still, and each edge has a handle of its own.** This reverses the 2026-09-16 choice to size about the centre, at the author's request: building a layout is mostly lengthening a module against a neighbour, and a resize that moves the face you are not touching undoes the abutment you just made. Both objections recorded then are smaller than they looked. The grid one is answered by snapping the *size* rather than the position — a module whose faces were on the half-metre grid keeps them there, and faces are what neighbours abut on; that its middle may land on a quarter metre matters to nothing. The shared-part one is answered by moving the module *within its assembly* rather than moving the copy: every copy grows the same way in its own frame, so the copy being dragged keeps its opposite face exactly and a mirrored twin keeps its mirrored face — widening one retro thruster outward widens the other outward too, rather than into the hull. An edge changes one dimension and leaves the other, and its middle, alone; edges are drawn as squares so the two kinds read differently. A thruster needs no special case — its position is its mounting face, so dragging the nozzle end moves nothing and dragging the mounting face moves the mounting. |
| 2026-09-24 | **A field the kind does not read is kept rather than cleared — dormant in memory, absent from the file.** Refitting a module from one archetype to another used to delete what stopped applying, because `moduleProblem` *refused* a spec carrying such a field: leaving one behind did not make an odd ship, it made every refit out of that kind impossible, silently, since a refused candidate is simply retried. But deleting throws away what a lineage learned: twenty generations of tuning a bell are gone the moment the module spends a generation as a gun mount, and a refit back starts from the default. **The value is now kept on the module, doing nothing, until a refit wakes it** — the dormant DNA the operator set was missing. Measured over 240 lineages of 120 generations from four founders: 5,627 refits, 1,718 of them landing a module on a value its new kind reads, and 0.41 dormant fields per module still held at the end — so the memory is used rather than hoarded, and does not grow without bound, each module having only four fields that can sleep: `nozzle`, `barrels`, `weapon` and a weapon's `traverse`. **Where the strictness went is the interesting half.** `moduleProblem` now checks a dormant value's *range* and nothing else about it, since the point of keeping one is that it is ready to be used; the honesty is enforced at the file layer instead. `serialiseBlueprint` writes only the fields the kind reads, so nothing a save produces can carry one, and `blueprintFileProblem` refuses a hand-written `nozzle` on a turret, or a `traverse` on a girder, the way it already refuses a key it does not know — a file is read by people and written once, so a ship there must say exactly what it is. That is a deliberate exception to the split the parser otherwise keeps (readability in the file layer, design rules in `blueprintProblem`): a dormant field is readable and is still refused, because a file claiming a module is something it is not is the failure that split exists to prevent, not an example of it. The editor cannot open such a file to fix it, which is the cost, and it is bounded by nothing being able to *write* one. |
| 2026-09-24 | **Every weapon carries the gear that trains it, and a layout may say how much arc to build for.** A mount's mass was its barrels and the machinery that loads them — nothing at all for the ring, the drive and the bearings, as though the gun were pointed by hand. `TRAVERSE_GEAR_FRACTION` prices it at a tenth of what actually swings, which is the whole mount for a turret that turns bodily and the barrels alone for a hull weapon whose block is welded to the ship. **The new `traverse` field is a limit rather than a capability**: asking for more than the mount can do changes nothing, and the ship being in the way still applies on top. What it *costs* is where the archetypes part, and the difference is the machine and not a rule. A turret's ring goes all the way round whatever it is told, so a limit on one is programming and weighs the same to the gram. A hull weapon's bed is built for the arc it sweeps, so its gear falls away with the arc and reaches **exactly zero** when the mount is told to train nothing — a gun welded to the ship, which is how a light hull affords a heavy bore. Measured: a hull gun on an 8x4 mount goes 42.88 t free, 40.11 t fixed, and against a 5x4 turret's 25.59 t it carries 400 mm to the turret's 160 and five times the muzzle energy; on a light hull a fixed 4x2 hull gun is 14.38 t for 200 mm and 8.8 MJ, where a 3x2 turret is 11.01 t for 80 mm and 2.1 MJ — so the fixed mount is the way a small ship carries a gun worth firing. The fleet pays for the honesty: every ship gains 1–3% dry mass (corvette +1.3%, gunship +2.8%), and eleven golden checksums move. Mutation gets the knob, in whole degrees, so a run can find fixed guns for itself rather than being given them. `ModuleStats.traverseMass` names the gear separately, because it is the one part of a mount its *arc* decides rather than its bore, and the editor reads it out as what the mount trains through and what that machine weighs. |
| 2026-09-24 | **One `nozzle` field for every module with something sticking out of it, rather than a second field meaning the same thing.** A hull mount's `muzzle` and a thruster's `nozzle` were the same quantity written twice — what share of the module's length is the part in front, the rest being the block that holds the machinery and takes the welds. Two fields meant two defaults, two validation rules, two mutation knobs and two operators to keep in step, for one idea. It is now one field, exactly as `barrels` already counts a turret's barrels, a hull mount's outlets and a thruster's nozzles: the *name* is the one thing that differs per archetype, so the editor labels the row bell, barrel or lens for the kind it is showing and the file format keeps one key. **`nozzle` is the key that survived** because it is the one already written into shipped blueprints — the Star Destroyer and the X-Wing both carry it — and renaming a field in the format costs every file ever saved, which is the same reason `barrels` was never renamed. Mutation loses a knob and an operator with it: `rebell` now moves whichever protrusion the module has, held off the far end harder on a weapon (all barrel leaves nothing to load from) than on an engine (no bell at all is a rocket whose nozzle has fallen off, which is legal and bad). The refit rule simplifies with it — a field that is refused on the kind being refitted *to* must be cleared or the refit is silently impossible — and now reads as one line covering both kinds that have a protrusion rather than two that overlap. |
| 2026-09-23 | **How hard a nozzle is fed is the machinery's business, so an engine's best bell is in the middle rather than at an end.** Thrust was the exit face's area and the bell's divergence and nothing else, so the machinery block was priced and bought nothing: a longer bell was always a better bell, and the only thing stopping an engine being all nozzle was running out of block to bolt to. What actually limits the flow is the chamber and the pumps — which are the block the bell was cut out of — so a shallow bell leaves a deep chamber and drives more gas through the same throat, and a deep one leaves an engine with nothing behind it. The throat **chokes** rather than passing whatever is pushed at it (`THROAT_CHOKE`), which is what stops a long thin engine being unbounded thrust for the price of being long. The two pull opposite ways, so thrust peaks inside the range and both ends are bad, which is what a knob should do. Machinery mass follows the flow rather than the face, since pumps are sized by what goes through them. **`PUMP_DEPTH_WIDTHS` is calibrated against the engines people actually draw, and that is the whole of what the constant decides.** A reference depth of one width — a rocket's proportions — starves every ship in the fleet to about a third of its thrust, which is not an opinion about the law but a measurement: nine behavioural tests fail, the ordering scenario's ships never cross the 1,800 m they start apart (97 rounds fired and 5 hits against 144 and 125), and the gunship out-accelerates the corvette because its 8x4 engine is well proportioned and the corvette's 4x6 is not. At a third of a width the fleet flies as it did — corvette 4.36 m/s² against the gunship's 4.13, and turning 4.8 times faster — and what the pump term does is punish a bell longer than about two thirds of the module rather than reward a chamber. Getting the reward as well needs either a higher choke or a fleet whose engines are drawn deeper, and that is a decision about the ships rather than about the law. Eleven of fourteen golden checksums move; `orbit`, `tumble` and `gunnery` do not, having no engine in them. The plume follows the thrust, so the flame now runs out at the same interior optimum — the reach test says that instead of "longer bell, longer flame", and the burn test's victim stands two metres behind the nozzle rather than ten. |
| 2026-09-23 | **An engine is bolted on like any other module: the rule that it needed structure against a particular face is gone.** It was written when a thruster *was* a nozzle — a mounting with a flame on the end of it, which had to be held on by the end it pushed from because there was nothing else to hold. An engine now carries its own machinery block, so it is a module with a bell on the back rather than a bell with a bolt on the front, and there is nothing left for the rule to protect: a block welded to a gun mount is as welded as one welded to a girder. **What remains is the rule every module obeys** — it has to be attached to the ship, through its neighbours or directly — and that already refuses an engine floating in space beside the hull, which is the case worth refusing. The rest the rule used to catch is now a *design* question with a physical answer rather than a legality: an engine mounted with its exhaust into its own hull burns what it is pointed at and gets no thrust for the gas it fires into itself, so the layout is simply bad and the plume says how bad, continuously, instead of a rule saying no. That is the same trade §12 recorded for a *blocked* nozzle, now applied to the mounting as well, and it removes the last place where the layout rules had an opinion about engines that the physics did not. It also removes the asymmetry that made an engine the one module whose legality depended on which way round it was drawn — a thing a search has to learn by being refused, rather than by being worse. `hullAgainst` and `MOUNTING_FACES` go with it, and the tests that pinned the refusals now pin the acceptances. |
| 2026-09-23 | **A hull beam's recovery is a fixed time, so a deeper mount fires for more of the battle rather than merely for longer at a stretch.** The bank is in the block and a flat `BEAM_DUTY_CYCLE` divided it: twice the block bought twice the burn and twice the wait, so **average power on target was exactly the same 6.28 MW at every proportion** and the knob did nothing but change how the same energy was parcelled out. The physical answer falls out of the same volume the bank is measured in: the plant that refills it and the sinks that dump what the shot made are the same machinery, so the recharge *time* is stored energy over a rate that both scale with volume — the volume cancels and the recovery is a property of the technology, not of the mount. Burn therefore grows with depth and recovery does not, which is the whole of why depth is worth buying. `BEAM_RECHARGE_TIME` is calibrated so a hull beam of the proportions an unsaid layout gets works at about the quarter duty the stop-gap assumed, so this refines that constant where it applies rather than moving the balance out from under it: on an 8x4 mount the duty runs 0.384, 0.344, 0.247, 0.164, 0.061 at shares 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9, and average power 9.64, 8.64, 6.20, 4.13 and 1.54 MW. It also gives mount *shape* a meaning it did not have: duty follows the block's aspect ratio, so a 16x4 mount holds 0.396 against an 8x8's 0.141, and a squat wide mount is a heavy burst weapon while a deep narrow one is sustained fire. **The knob is deliberately one-sided on a beam and that is worth saying plainly**: the aperture comes from the mount's width, so the lens housing buys nothing at all and the best housing is the smallest one that will hold the optic. What would make it a trade is the housing buying focus — §12 already holds spot size, wavelength and what armour does about them, and this is another reason to settle it. `BEAM_DUTY_CYCLE` still governs beam *turrets*, for the reason hull guns' loading law does not cover gun turrets: it would move every shipped ship. |
| 2026-09-23 | **The block behind a hull gun's barrel is its loading gear, so it sets the rate of fire.** The archetype's prose already called the block the breech and the loading machinery, and the arithmetic ignored it: `cycleTime` came from calibre alone, calibre comes from the mount's *width*, so a hull gun's whole rate of fire was set by its opening and the block was worth nothing but hit points. That left `muzzle` a two-way trade — velocity against traverse — with the third thing the split obviously means going unpriced. The depth is counted in **calibres of the round** rather than in metres, so the same proportions mean the same rate at any size, and `LOADING_BLOCK_CALIBRES` is set to what half an 8x4 mount comes to at the bore it carries: a gun whose layout says nothing about its proportions loads at exactly a turret's rate for its calibre, and the knob moves either way from there rather than up or down from it. **`LOADING_FLOOR` is the quarter of a cycle no machinery shortens** — breech, ram, run-out — and it is there because rate of fire is named in §12 as the figure an evolved design would run away with: without it a long mount with a stub barrel is an unbounded autocannon, and with it two hundred times the expected depth still buys under four times the rate. What the law buys is an **interior optimum**: on an 8x4 mount, sustained muzzle energy runs 41, 147, 264, 267, 199 MJ/min at shares 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.6 and 0.8 — so the best gun is a little over half barrel, and both ends of the knob are genuinely bad. A turret's mount is the same machinery and is not covered: its barrel is derived rather than authored, so the law would arrive there as a side effect of size rather than as a choice, and it would move every shipped ship's rate of fire. ROADMAP.md §12 records that asymmetry to settle with the fleet's balance rather than ahead of it. |

| 2026-09-23 | **A saved ship shadows a shipped one for opening by name, and is listed beside it rather than over it.** Saving over a shipped name was already a shadow rather than a replacement — deleting the saved copy brought the original back — but the list showed one entry per *name*, so the only route back to the shipped hull was to destroy the player's copy of it. | 2026-09-23 | **Whether a blueprint file can be *read* and whether it describes a ship that could *fly* are separate questions, and only the first is the parser's.** `blueprintFileProblem` checked the file's shape and then handed the assembled layout to `blueprintProblem`, so a file naming an overlapping hull, a missing core or an engine welded to nothing was refused outright — and since the editor opens a saved ship through the same parser, **the one tool that could have fixed such a ship was the one place it could not be opened**. That is the worst property a rule can have: it bites exactly when something is already wrong. It bit on layouts saved before a rule existed or before a convention moved — a thruster's position became the middle of the face it pushes from rather than the centre of its box, which reads an older save as an engine floating free of the hull — so ships that were valid when they were written became unopenable rather than merely faulty. `parseBlueprint` now refuses only what it genuinely cannot read: unknown keys, a format version from the future, a length that is not a number. Nothing is lost by moving the design rules out of it, because **`compileBlueprint` already refuses to fly an invalid layout** and is the gate every battle goes through; validity is enforced where a ship is built rather than where a file is read. The editor consequently opens a broken ship, draws it, and names every fault in the problems panel — which is what the panel is for, and it already showed exactly these faults for a layout being dragged through an invalid state. **Saving and exporting a faulty layout are allowed for the same reason**: the gate on them said "a file with them cannot be read back", which was true and is not any more, and work in progress is the normal state of a layout being drawn. A file that cannot be parsed at all is still refused, and the editor now survives one rather than failing to start: it says which saved ship it could not read and carries on with a blank one. |
| 2026-09-23 | **Every archetype is listed once, in `MODULE_KINDS`.** Adding `hullGun` and `hullBeam` had to be done in four places before a run could breed one — the file format's validation, a run's mutation weights, the weighted draw itself and the flattener's sort order — and missing any of them fails in a way that looks like something else: a perfectly good ship refused by a loader, or a kind the operator is told it may build and never does. | 2026-09-04 | Thruster allocation built. Three findings worth keeping: the torque row must be **preconditioned** (mount arms are metres, so torque outweighs force by ~10⁴ in the normal equations, and once thrusters pin the solve quietly fits torque and ignores force — 75% shortfall on achievable demands); the normal equations must **switch form** with the number of free thrusters, `A Aᵀ` when underdetermined and `Aᵀ A` when fewer than three remain, since the 3×3 form is singular there; and only the **worst violator** may be pinned per pass, because pinning all of them collapses a nine-thruster layout to two and never reconsiders. Together these took the worst-case shortfall from >100% to 5.4%, mean 0.016%. |
| 2026-09-03 | Tightened the commit model (§3), which had two holes. A craft could commit while already over a capital's interior and strike the citadel without meeting the armoured edge; and a craft that *moved* to the hull layer would stop being hittable by CIWS and lasers exactly when it should be most exposed. Fixed by making occupancy *added, never swapped* — a committed craft is in both layers — and by allowing occupancy to change only while clear of every hull, in either direction, which also governs waving off and gives "commit is arming" for free. Corrected the earlier claim that the transition is an event rather than a state: true of projectiles, false once commit exists, which is one bit per craft. Also corrected the unearned claim that committing draws more fire — every weapon is weapons-layer, so the real costs are a predictable terminal course and being collidable with turrets. |
